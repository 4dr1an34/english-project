"My Son the Fanatic" — Summary

Part 1: The Bedroom & Discarded Possessions (pp. 19–20)

Parvez discovers that his son Ali has cleared out his room. Ali throws away fashionable clothes, computer disks, books, and his electric guitar, stating: "There are more important things to be done." Ali parts with his English girlfriend and abandons sports and his accounting studies. Ashamed, Parvez begins drinking whisky at work.

Part 2: Drug Fears, Bettina & Night Prayers (pp. 21–23)

Suspecting drugs, Parvez seeks advice from his friend Bettina, a prostitute. She tells him "It's all in the eyes". Ali's eyes are clear, but he is growing a beard. At night, Parvez catches Ali praying five times daily facing Mecca. This triggers Parvez's childhood trauma in Lahore, where a strict Quran teacher tied his hair to the ceiling, making him hate religious dogma.

Part 3: The Restaurant Confrontation (pp. 24–28)

Parvez takes Ali to an Indian restaurant to reconnect. Ali refuses to eat and condemns Parvez's drinking and pork consumption ("This is England. We have to fit in"). Ali declares: "Western materialists hate us... There will be jihad." On the drive home, Ali reveals he dropped out of college because Western accounting involves sinful interest ("usury").

Part 4: Kitchen Philosophy & Cab Ride (pp. 29–32)

At the kitchen table, Parvez explains his humanist philosophy: "Enjoy yourself without hurting others." Ali rejects enjoyment as "a bottomless pit". Days later, Bettina rides in Parvez's cab and tries to defend Parvez to Ali. Ali insults her with venom ("a woman like you"). Humiliated, Bettina leaps from the moving taxi into the road.

Part 5: The Violent Climax & Final Words (p. 32)

Parvez drives Ali home in silence, drinks downstairs in despair, and storms into Ali's prayer room. In a drunken rage, Parvez beats his son bloody. Ali neither flinches nor fights back, calmly asking the final question: "So who's the fanatic now?"

Part 6: Acculturation, Author's Message & Abitur Context

• Acculturation: Parvez chose Assimilation (secularism), Ali chose Rejection (fundamentalism). Their opposite paths make dialogue impossible.
• Author's Message: Fanaticism exists on both sides; secular people can become just as violent and intolerant as religious dogmatists.
• Abitur Theme: Illustrates 1st vs. 2nd generation immigrant identity crises and the limits of the British "melting pot".

Pages 19–20 // Sudden Change

Secret Changes in the Bedroom

Parvez, a London taxi driver for 20 years, secretly inspects Ali's bedroom. Expecting typical teenage clutter, he is shocked to find the room stripped bare and neatly ordered.

"What bewildered him was that Ali was getting tidier. He was sorting out his belongings surreptitiously."

By the dustbin, Parvez finds Ali's discarded clothes, computer disks, and electric guitar. Ali breaks up with his English girlfriend, stating: "There are more important things to be done."

Page 20 // The Father's Shame

The Forgotten Cricket Bat

On the walls, only pale marks remain where posters hung. In the corner, Ali's cricket bat gathers dust—he has abandoned sports and dropped his accounting studies.

Parvez worked endless night shifts to afford Ali's education and used to boast about him to Punjabi colleagues. Now deeply ashamed, Parvez starts drinking whisky at work for the first time.

Pages 21–22 // Drug Fears

"It's All in the Eyes"

Fellow taxi drivers suspect Ali is selling possessions to buy drugs. Terrified, Parvez asks advice from Bettina, a prostitute and close confidante he once rescued from a violent client.

"It's all in the eyes."

Bettina tells him how to spot drug use. When Ali comes home, his eyes are clear, steady, and alert—ruling out drugs completely.

Page 23 // Discovery & Trauma

Night Prayers & Lahore Trauma

Peeking through Ali's bedroom door at night, Parvez sees Ali growing a beard and praying on the floor facing Mecca: "I feel as if I've lost my son."

This triggers Parvez's childhood trauma in Lahore, where an abusive Quran teacher tied his hair to the ceiling so he would be jerked awake if he fell asleep. Since that abuse, Parvez has despised strict religion.

Pages 24–26 // Ideological Duel

A Fight Across the Dinner Table

Parvez takes Ali to an Indian restaurant to connect. But Ali refuses food and drinks, attacking Parvez for drinking alcohol and eating crispy bacon and pork sausages ("This is England. We have to fit in").

"Western materialists hate us... Papa, how can you love something which hates you?"

Ali warns of hellfire and states: "There will be jihad." Furious and desperate, Parvez orders double whiskies and smashes a plate on the floor.

Pages 27–28 // The Ride Home

"That is the Problem"

Driving home, Parvez defends England: "They let you do almost anything here." Ali coldly retorts: "That is the problem."

"I love England... They let you do almost anything here." — "That is the problem."

After Parvez clips a lorry, Ali refuses to help his injured father up. Ali confirms he quit college because Western accounting involves sinful interest ("usury"). Bettina urges Parvez: "Show him love."

Pages 29–30 // Kitchen Dialogue

Parvez's Humanist Plea

At the kitchen table, Parvez explains his personal worldview: life should be guided by mutual respect, love, and happiness.

"Grass and flowers will grow out of my grave, but something of me will live on... in you... Enjoy yourself without hurting others."

Ali rejects this with cold dogma, calling enjoyment "a bottomless pit": "Real morality has existed for hundreds of years. Millions share my beliefs."

Pages 30–32 // Confrontation in the Cab

Bettina's Escape into the Night

Parvez picks up Ali while Bettina sits in the cab. Bettina tries to plead on Parvez's behalf: "Your father... loves you more than his own life."

"Then why is he letting a woman like you touch him like that? ... You know what kind."

Deeply humiliated and frightened by Ali's venom, Bettina throws herself from the moving taxi into the road and runs away into the dark.

Book Page 32 // The Violent Climax

"So Who's the Fanatic Now?"

Parvez drives Ali home in stony silence. Downstairs, Parvez drinks heavily in helpless rage before storming into Ali's room, finding his son kneeling in prayer.

"Parvez kicked him over... dragged the boy up by the front of his shirt and hit him... The boy's face was bloody. Parvez was panting... The boy neither covered himself nor retaliated: there was no fear in his eyes."

Ali looks calmly at his father through his split lip and delivers the book's haunting final question:

"So who's the fanatic now?"
Literary Interpretation // Hanif Kureishi

The Author's Core Message

What Hanif Kureishi communicates through the climactic confrontation and the unresolved final question.

01 // CORE THEME Universal Principle

Fanaticism on Both Sides

Fanaticism is not exclusive to religion. Parvez claims to stand for Western freedom and tolerance, but when his son rejects his lifestyle, Parvez resorts to alcohol and brutal violence. Secularists can become just as fanatical, intolerant, and dogmatic as religious extremists.

02 // CORE THEME Social Warning

The Danger of Extremes

The story warns against two rigid extremes: blind assimilation (sacrificing your cultural roots to please the host society) and militant fundamentalism (rejecting the outside world with moral superiority). Both extremes destroy dialogue.

03 // CORE THEME Human Cost

Collapse of Human Empathy

When ideology replaces emotional connection, human relationships crumble. Ali shows callous contempt for his hardworking father and Bettina, while Parvez loses his composure and physically attacks his own child. Dogma eliminates compassion.

04 // CORE THEME Literary Device

The Open Ending & Reflection

By ending on an unanswered question ("So who's the fanatic now?"), Kureishi avoids moralizing. The question directly challenges the reader to reflect on who the true fanatic is, the boundaries of personal freedom, and the challenges of multicultural Britain.

Character Analysis // Pages 19–32

Character Profiles & Motivations

A structured analysis of the three characters, based directly on the events, actions, and dialogues in the book.

The Father (1st Generation)

Parvez

London taxi driver from Lahore, Pakistan

  • Hard Work & Paternal Love (pp. 19–20): Drove 20 years of night shifts without complaint to buy Ali nice suits and pay for his accounting studies. Parvez expresses love through material sacrifice.
  • Trauma & Secular Freedom (pp. 23, 26): Abused as a child by an abusive Lahore Quran teacher, he despises religious dogma. In England, he embraces personal freedom (whisky, bacon, sausages; "This is England. We have to fit in").
  • Tragic Loss of Control (p. 32): Deeply humiliated by Ali's rejection, Parvez tries to connect through love and philosophy. When words fail and Bettina is insulted, his desperation turns into drunken rage—becoming the violent fanatic himself.
The Son (2nd Generation)

Ali

British-born student turned fundamentalist

  • Former Integrated Life (pp. 19–20): Once a successful British teen: straight A's, college accounting student, cricket, football, electric guitar, and an English girlfriend.
  • Radicalisation & Rejection (pp. 26–29): Feeling alienated in Britain ("Western materialists hate us"), he views his father's assimilation as weak "grovelling". Strict religion restores his pride, dignity, and a sense of moral superiority ("he was not inferior").
  • The Role of Martyr (p. 32): Cold and merciless toward Bettina and Parvez. In taking Parvez's beating without defending himself, Ali wins the moral high ground and asks: "So who's the fanatic now?"
The Confidante

Bettina

Prostitute, Parvez's loyal friend and passenger

  • Loyal Confidante (pp. 21–22): Saved by Parvez from a violent customer 3 years ago. She sits in his front passenger seat and is the only person Parvez can truly confide in.
  • Voice of Empathy & Reason (pp. 22, 28): Highly perceptive. She teaches Parvez to check Ali's eyes for drugs and prevents him from kicking Ali out: "You have to show him love."
  • The Dramatic Catalyst (pp. 31–32): In the cab, she defends Parvez with affection. Humiliated and frightened by Ali's venom ("a woman like you"), she leaps from the moving taxi. Her flight shatters Parvez's last self-control.
Sociological Framework // Cultural Encounters

Acculturation & Abitur Context

The four cultural pathways (John Berry / Kamran Ahmed) and how the story connects directly to the Abitur topic "Cultural Encounters".

The 4 Acculturation Pathways

How Father & Son Navigate British Culture

Acculturation describes how immigrants balance their heritage culture with the host nation's culture. In the story, Parvez and Ali represent diametrically opposed outcomes:

1. Assimilation

Parvez (The Father)
Host: High · Origin: Low

Definition: Adopting the host culture while abandoning the culture and religion of origin.

Book Facts (pp. 19, 26–27): 20 years in London; drinks whisky, eats crispy bacon and pork sausages; insists "This is England. We have to fit in" (p. 26); avoids mosques; worked endless night shifts to buy Ali Western clothes and fund his accounting studies.

Deduction: Abused as a boy by a Quran teacher in Lahore (p. 23), Parvez sees religion as oppressive. To him, assimilation is freedom, progress, and safety for his family.

2. Integration

Ali's Former Life ("Old" Ali)
Host: High · Origin: High

Definition: Keeping ties to family heritage while actively participating and achieving success in the host society.

Book Facts (pp. 19–20): Lived with Pakistani parents while achieving full English success: top grades (straight A's), studying accounting, playing cricket, swimming, football, electric guitar, and having an English girlfriend.

Deduction: Integration seemed ideal, but was fragile. Ali felt that playing cricket and getting top grades did not stop British society from looking down on him ("Western materialists hate us", p. 26).

3. Deculturation

Parvez's Isolation (Rootlessness)
Host: Low · Origin: Low

Definition: Losing connection to one's culture of origin without ever truly being accepted in the host country, leading to alienation.

Book Facts (pp. 20–24, 29, 32): Cut off from Pakistani religion (p. 23), but remains an overworked cabbie on the social sidelines. When Ali calls him a "groveler" (p. 29), Parvez sits downstairs alone, drinking in total silence and isolation (p. 32).

Deduction: Parvez has no religious roots, no community support, and is rejected by his son. This total rootlessness causes his emotional breakdown and violent outburst.

4. Rejection

Ali's Fundamentalism ("New" Ali)
Host: Low · Origin: High / Radical

Definition: Completely rejecting host culture and retreating into strict religious orthodoxy or a militant counter-culture.

Book Facts (pp. 19–20, 26–32): Purges room into dustbin (clothes, guitar, video tapes, p. 19); breaks up with English girlfriend; quits university (p. 28); condemns enjoyment as "a bottomless pit" (p. 30); insults Bettina (p. 31); warns: "There will be jihad" (p. 26).

Deduction: Fundamentalism gives Ali pride and moral superiority ("he was not inferior", p. 29). Enduring Parvez's beating without flinching cements his role as an unyielding martyr (p. 32).

Curriculum Link // Abitur English

Connection to the Topic: "Cultural Encounters"

  • 1. 1st vs. 2nd Generation Immigrant Conflict:
    • 1st Generation (Parvez): Post-colonial immigrants who endured hard labor and discrimination to achieve the "British Dream." They prioritize economic survival, adaptation, and material success.
    • 2nd Generation (Ali): Born in the UK, but caught between two worlds. Feeling subtle racism and Western hostility ("Western materialists hate us", p. 26), Ali rejects his father's assimilation as weak "grovelling" (p. 29) and turns to fundamentalism for pride and belonging.
  • 2. Multicultural Britain & Post-Colonial Legacy:
    Kureishi explores the limits of the British "melting pot" (contrasting with optimistic views like Benjamin Zephaniah's "The British"). The story shows how historical imperial tensions and social exclusion can push marginalized youth away from integration and into radicalisation.
  • 3. The Tragedy of Failed Cultural Encounters:
    In the short story curriculum (alongside George Orwell's "Shooting an Elephant" and Zadie Smith's "The Embassy of Cambodia"), My Son the Fanatic serves as the central example of how cultural encounters fail when dialogue collapses, empathy is lost, and rigid dogmatism replaces mutual respect.
